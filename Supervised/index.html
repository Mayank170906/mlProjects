Project 01 · Supervised learning

Predicting customer dissatisfaction before the review lands.

A marketplace learns an order went wrong when the one-star review arrives — days after anything could have been done about it. This pipeline scores every order using only signals available before the customer writes anything, turning a post-mortem metric into an operational queue.

The finding

Lateness is not one factor among many. It is the factor.

Every feature in the model competes to explain a review score. One wins by a wide margin, and it is the one operations can actually control.

On-time deliveries

◆ What this buys you. The model does not need to guess at sentiment. It needs to combine a reliable lateness signal with the seller, category and freight context that predicts which shipments will run late — which is exactly what the top features do.

Approach

Five stages, each with a decision worth defending.

Relational ETL across seven tables

Customers, orders, items, products, sellers, payments and reviews joined on a star schema with orders at the centre. Row count moves at every step and each move is explained: items fan an order out, payments fan it out again, missing reviews contract it.

99,441 → — rows 39 columns

Target definition without leakage

is_satisfied = review_score >= 4. Everything derived from the review — its text, its title, its timestamps — is dropped, along with order_delivered_carrier_date and the raw score itself. What remains is knowable at dispatch.

Reviews-derived columns dropped

34 features, aggregates fitted post-split

Row-level engineering (delay severity, freight ratio, price per volume, purchase hour) is safe anywhere. Seller and category track records are not: they are computed on training rows only, then joined onto validation rows with a train-derived fallback for unseen keys.

34 features 5 native categoricals

Honest limits

What this model cannot do.

  • It cannot see the product. No image, no description quality, no returns history. A large share of one-star reviews are about the item itself and are structurally invisible here.
  • Dissatisfaction recall is —. Close to half of unhappy customers are still missed at the published threshold; moving the cut trades that against a much larger false-positive queue.
  • The split is random, not temporal. Orders from 2018 can inform predictions about 2017. A production system should be validated forward in time.
  • Seller aggregates are cold-start fragile. New sellers fall back to the global mean and lose the model's most useful contextual signal.

Next

Where it goes from here.

  • Replace the random split with a rolling time-based backtest and report the drift.
  • Add geodesic customer–seller distance from the geolocation table, currently unused.
  • Mine the Portuguese review text of historical orders into a seller-level quality prior — legitimate, since it uses prior orders rather than the current one.
  • Wrap the ONNX model in a scoring service and monitor the prediction distribution against the training-time distribution.
